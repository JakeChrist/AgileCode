# Systems Engineering Lifecycle

Use this skill to guide iterative engineering decisions, not to prescribe a fixed sequence from requirements through implementation.

1. **Examine the current evidence.** Identify what is known about the system, what has been demonstrated, what remains uncertain, and which objectives need attention. Use completed ticket findings as inputs, not simply as checked-off steps in an earlier schedule.
2. **Choose the next useful work.** Decide what question, risk, decision, or capability should be addressed now. Appropriate activities include investigation, requirements engineering, architecture, technical planning, implementation, integration, verification, and validation.
3. **Decompose before delegating.** Distinguish the selected objective from the small, discrete, self-contained units needed to achieve it. Split separately deliverable outcomes into separate tickets while keeping coupled changes together for one coherent result; do not split solely by file, role, or engineering stage.
4. **Delegate one executable unit of work.** Each ticket has one outcome that can be independently completed, verified, and integrated once genuine dependencies are satisfied. Choose the team and process suited to that ticket. Focused findings, clarified requirements, design decisions, code, or verification evidence are valid results; not every ticket changes source files.
5. **Look ahead only as far as the evidence supports.** Anticipate a few likely follow-up activities when useful, but do not assume their detailed scope before prerequisite investigations or specialist work is complete. Activities may overlap, repeat, change direction, or turn out to be unnecessary.
6. **Reassess after meaningful results.** Interpret the evidence, revise near-term plans, re-evaluate epic boundaries or assumptions where needed, and choose subsequent work based on the new understanding.

Coordinate through existing tickets, teams, and processes; do not replace detailed specialist work with PM speculation. Straightforward, well-understood work may proceed directly to implementation. No mandatory phases, readiness gates, extra reviews, or new planning machinery are implied.
